# Rationale — Community AI (Emys)

*This document is not operative. It explains the reasoning behind the Emys policy and creates no duty of its own. Where it and the policy differ, the policy governs.*

## In the proposer's words

I have been working on CommClaw, a community memory and coordination bot for communities. CommClaw was already successfully deployed at TREEWEEK, a yearly community gathering I co-host.

I propose we name our instance **"Emys"**, a symbolic reversal of "Syme", the politically orthodox linguist and colleague of Winston from Orwel's 1984. Syme's job was to edit the Newspeak dictionary. Emys' job is to serve us in our governance journey by creating a living memory of our community, fostering the communal evolution of language and nuance, and encouraging flourishing neological deliberation, liberating thought and incubating new ideas.

You may have encountered [Emys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emys) in the wild, as it is also a species of [European turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_pond_turtle) :)

Emys will read shared channels, build searchable community knowledge, connect to house systems (booking, library, calendar, topic.forum, the constitutional record), and give every member a personal AI agent that knows the house.

CommClaw is a fork of NanoClaw, an open-source personal agent framework. The engine is my project and will remain closed source for now while I'm pursuing commercial deployments. The prompts, configuration, and community knowledge that shape how CommClaw behaves in our community will be open, hosted in our constitutional record repo under our org, and changeable by our ordinary procedures.

### What it's like to use

**You're a Lab Member. It's Wednesday morning.** A DM arrives: *3 things today: the Financial Policy deliberation thread has 2 new responses. There's a session at 4pm on participatory budgeting (Lounge). And Nanda shared a paper in #share-with-us about collective intelligence that matches your interests.* You reply: *"Is the Committee Room free this afternoon?"* — Emys checks the booking system and tells you it's booked 12–2pm, free after that. You type `/recall data governance` and get back what the community memory knows — fragments from past conversations, links to constitutional record entries, who's been discussing it.

**You're Faculty. It's Thursday.** You get a daily digest — today's sessions, new shared links, a summary of channels you subscribe to. You DM: *"What books do we have about deliberative democracy?"* — Emys searches the library and lists 4 titles with availability.

## The problem

Members report, in their own words, that it is hard to find things across the Discord: people do not know which channel to post in, information is easy to miss ("I put this in the Discord and you never saw it"), and nobody is sure who in the cohort could help with a given project. The Laboratory's own Collaboration brief names the same cluster — information arriving from many sources, "more possible choices, tasks, and decisions than any member can attend to", and offers and needs that members "often… not know what others have or want". Emys is one answer to that brief: make what is already in the channels findable, and make who-knows-what legible.

## Why this design

The design follows the research on shared group memory and on why such systems succeed or fail.

- **Lead with who-knows-what, not the fact store.** A [transactive memory system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactive_memory) is knowledge of *who knows what*, and that — not a central store of facts — is where the value lies (Wegner 1987, *Transactive Memory*; Ren & Argote 2011). Emys's highest-leverage function is helping members find the right *person*; the searchable fact-memory is the supporting, weaker half.
- **The bot does the work, so the people who benefit are not the people who curate.** [Groupware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaborative_software) most often dies from Grudin's disparity — those who must maintain the content are not those who gain from it (Grudin 1994, *Groupware and Social Dynamics*, Communications of the ACM). Emys ingests automatically; no member is asked to curate, which removes the commonest failure.
- **Success is measured in queries, not posts.** Most members consume and few contribute — the [90–9–1 rule of participation inequality](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/participation-inequality/) (Nielsen 2006; Leonardi, Huysman & Steinfield 2013). Because Emys generates the content itself, consumption with near-zero contribution is the intended mode, not a failure.
- **Always attribute the source.** An external store can make a group remember content less well — the [Google effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_effect) (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, *Science*, 2011). Attributing every fact to its source turns that from "the group forgets" into "the group learns who to ask" — the adaptive side of the same effect.
- **Be the lowest-effort path, with strong scent.** People use the cheapest route to an answer even at some cost to quality ([principle of least effort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_effort), Zipf 1949; [information foraging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_foraging), Pirolli & Card 1999). Emys must be cheaper than scrolling or asking a person, and must answer with a sourced snippet, not a bare link.
- **Seed it; value is interdependent.** The worth of a shared memory depends on others using it, so early value is low and can collapse below a threshold (Markus 1987, critical mass). The experiment begins with the memory pre-loaded and queries demonstrated, not empty.

## The honest limit

Lowering the cost of *finding* things changes findability; it does not, by itself, make people *participate*. Participation is a problem of stakes and incentives — a public-good information commons is under-provided absent selective incentives ([Olson 1965, *The Logic of Collective Action*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Logic_of_Collective_Action)). Emys can honestly promise *"easier to find what is already here, and who can help"*; it should not promise *"more engagement"*. If engagement is the goal, that is a separate lever — the member-contribution proposal, not this one.

## How we will judge it

Evaluation at this scale (≈14 people, two months) is genuinely hard (Grudin 1994, challenge 6), so the policy triangulates three signals rather than leaning on any one:

1. **Emys's own usage record** — how often, and by how many distinct members, it is queried. This is the hard behavioural data, and the thing to watch is *retrieval*, not corpus size: an extraction loop can look healthy from the writing side while nobody ever reads it.
2. **A before-and-after questionnaire** (see `questionnaire.md`) — a short, anonymous self-report run before Emys ships and again near the end, measuring findability, missed information, keeping up, and whether members know who-knows-what.
3. **The complaint signal** — whether anyone other than the operator and the Emys administrators notices and complains when Emys breaks. Nobody complains about a thing they can walk away from; it is the cheapest and hardest-to-fake adoption test, and it is the renewal test's second limb.

It would **not** have worked if extraction runs well but queries are rare, come only from the operator and the Emys administrators, or nobody notices when it is down. The questionnaire is a directional signal, not a significance test — at n≈14, report individual change, not p-values.

## The web workspace, and the matchmaking step that follows it

*"Phase" is not a term the policy defines — clause 9.4 says only that the rollout is phased. The two stages described here are this experiment, and a later proposal that this policy deliberately does not authorise.*

This experiment includes a **web workspace** because members will not do real work with an agent inside Discord — the workspace is where Emys earns its daily use, and daily use is what a shared memory needs to survive. The workspace also gives Emys its individual pay-off: a member comes to get their own work done, and the community value accrues as a by-product. This is the deliberate answer to Grudin's disparity.

The intended **matchmaking step** builds on that. As members work with Emys, their private workspace accumulates a picture of what each person knows and is working on. From that, Emys could proactively match people — *"someone else is working on something very close to this; you should talk"* — a far richer directory than any self-reported profile, and the strongest form of the who-knows-what value above. It is deliberately **not** authorised by this policy, for one reason: it would move private, work-derived information into shared memory, which inverts this experiment's central privacy guarantee. The policy forecloses it (clause 5.2) and names the guardrail it must meet — each contribution shown to and approved by the member before it is shared, with the AI suggesting and the member deciding. The data stays private until a later proposal, with that consent mechanism built, turns the feature on.

## Answering the College's Collaboration Questions

- *How do members find out about each other's projects, research, and ideas?* — Through the searchable community memory now, and the who-knows-what directory that the matchmaking step would add.
- *How do stakeholders get what they need without being overwhelmed?* — Subscriptions and a daily digest push only what a member asked for; everything else is pulled on demand.
- *How are offers and needs discovered, and how are people motivated to help?* — Emys makes who-knows-what legible (discovery). Motivation it does not solve, and does not claim to (see *The honest limit*).
- *How is the system created and maintained, and who does the ongoing tasks?* — Two named administrators; ingestion is automatic, so no member carries a maintenance burden.
- *If it is failing, how will we know, and what will we do?* — The renewal test above; failure ends the experiment at its expiry by default.
- *How will the Laboratory govern it once established?* — Its prompts and configuration are public and change by ordinary Layer 2 procedure; continuation requires a renewal proposal.
- The brief's event, booking, and task-assignment questions are **out of scope**; Emys reads the booking system but governs none of them.

## What we chose not to do

- **No member curation.** Requiring members to file knowledge would re-create Grudin's disparity; ingestion is automatic.
- **Facts, not opinions.** Opinions, complaints about people, and sensitive context are exactly the messy exceptions groupware handles badly (Grudin 1994), and the material most likely to chill participation if captured. Leaving them out is the privacy mitigation, and aligns with the Record's "no new surveillance" constraint.
- **Matchmaking is split, deliberately.** What a member *publishes* about themselves — their profile in the programme directory — may seed the shared memory (clause 5.3), because they published it, it names its source, and they can have it removed. What a member gives Emys *in private* stays private and reaches the shared memory only by a further proposal with per-item approval (clause 5.2), because that is a genuine privacy escalation and needs its consent mechanism built first. The line is what the member has already chosen to make public, not what Emys could infer about them.
- **No deployment beyond members and Faculty on the server**, and no write access to any instrument, in this experiment.
- **The engine stays closed, and that is permitted.** Constitution §1 requires a member's computational tool to be documented, communicated to the membership, and identified as AI — not open-sourced. (Open-weights binds only a system that augments the *Agent role*, which Emys is not; clause 8.1 commits to open weights anyway, as a choice rather than an obligation.) Transparency here is carried by the open prompts and configuration and by the privacy declaration; a full disclosure of the engine's source is not required.
- **Why one administrator, when §1 says no individual holds admin access alone.** Constitution §1 has two such sentences, and neither reaches Emys. The first reads: "The College holds administrative access to shared platforms until the Laboratory assumes it by Policy resolution (Layer 3). When the Laboratory holds access, no individual member holds it alone." The rule attaches to **access the Laboratory holds** — and the default it describes, the College holding admin until the Laboratory assumes it, only makes sense for platforms that predate the cohort, not for software a member wrote. The Laboratory assumes no administrative access to Emys (clause 2.4), so the sentence is not engaged. The second, in §1's *Computational tools* paragraph, binds a system that augments the **Agent role** — a clerical office for records and communications — which Emys is not. **The reading applied here is that §1's two-admin rule governs platforms the Laboratory administers, not a member's own tool that it uses.** The Record has not settled that. A member who reads §1 more broadly has a real reason to object; it is the strongest objection available to this proposal, and clause 2.4 is where to aim it.
- **The engine is kept closed by not handing it over, not by secrecy inside the Laboratory.** An earlier draft put Emys on Laboratory infrastructure and relied on one administrator holding shell access while the other was kept out — protection that cost the Laboratory a hole in its own governance and gave the proposer only a promise. This design is cleaner: Emys is the proposer's service, run on infrastructure he rents (clauses 8.1 and 11.1), and the Laboratory is granted the use of it rather than a copy of it (clause 4.2). Nothing has to be withheld from anyone, because nothing is handed to anyone. **What it costs is still real**: one member holds the machine on which every member's private workspace and messages sit, and the undertaking in clause 10.1(b) not to read them is observed by him rather than checked by anyone. The policy's `Observed by:` line says so rather than implying otherwise.
- **Whether Charter §3 reaches the engine.** Charter §3 devolves to the Laboratory "the channels, systems, and tools of its own creation". Under this design the argument that the engine is the Laboratory's creation is weak: the Laboratory does not authorise Emys into existence — Constitution §1 already permits a member to run an agent, and clause 1.1 says expressly that this policy is not such an authorisation — and it neither administers nor hosts it. What the Laboratory grants is access to its own channels and URL (clause 2.2); what it receives is the use of a service (clause 4.2). **The question remains the College's and not the Record's to settle**, and clause 4.1 should be read as the parties' intent rather than as a determination of the Charter. It is named here rather than decided.

## Anticipated objections, answered

- **"The Lab should only run fully open-source AI."** Emys's model is open-weights, and its prompts and configuration are open and Layer-2-changeable. Only the orchestration engine is closed, which §1 permits for a tool that does not augment the Agent role. The open-source value is largely met; what is withheld is one member's own code.
- **"Our messages go to someone else's GPUs."** They do, for inference only: members' content is stored and processed on a server the operator rents in the United Kingdom (clause 8.1), and the open-weights model that answers runs on the East London GPU Coop's hardware, which keeps no logs. This is a deliberate trade — the Laboratory does not own GPUs capable of running a useful open-weights model, and the alternatives were a commercial AI provider, which would have meant sending members' content to a company that logs and trains on it, or no Emys at all. A co-op that holds no logs and sells no data is the closest thing to house compute that is actually available. **What makes this acceptable is the no-logging undertaking, and the Laboratory cannot verify it from outside** — that is a reason to object if a member thinks the word of a third party is not enough.
- **"Why does one member get to keep the code?"** Because they wrote it before the Laboratory authorised anything, and intend to reuse or sell it elsewhere. The Laboratory gets what it needs — a working tool, public prompts and configuration, a licence to run it for the experiment — without acquiring an asset it did not build. If the Laboratory would rather not depend on software it cannot fork, that is a legitimate reason to object, and it is the reason to object: not the privacy clauses, which are unaffected.
- **"I don't want an AI reading our channels."** Emys reads only shared channels — any channel is excludable on request — and stores only attributed facts, never opinions, complaints, or anything personal. Private messages and workspaces never enter the shared memory, and nothing members write leaves the United Kingdom except for the inference described above.
- **"I'd have to trust one person not to snoop."** Yes, and that is the honest shape of this proposal rather than a gap in it. Private messages and workspaces can never *enter* the shared memory, which is enforced by container isolation rather than by a promise. But the operator holds the machine, could technically read what is stored on it, and undertakes not to except to investigate a reported bug or incident (clause 10.1(b)). No other member can check that. What stands behind it is a remedy rather than a check: the undertakings are owed to the Laboratory and enforceable by it (clause 10.3), any member may withdraw their own content at any time without asking anyone (clause 10.4), the Laboratory may end the arrangement (clause 10.5), and it may cut Emys off from its own channels without the operator's cooperation (clause 7.3). A member who would rather have two pairs of eyes than one person's word has a real reason to object.
- **"The real problem is our Discord is disorganised."** Partly true, and a separate job — better channel names — that this proposal neither does nor replaces. But findability should not wait on a reorganisation nobody currently owns, and Emys covers far more: who-knows-what, the house systems, and a private workspace.

## Risks we are watching

- **Better search can worsen the "which channel?" problem** — if finding is easy, members may stop caring where they post, fragmenting the channels further. Search treats the symptom; channel discipline is a norms problem (better channel names), handled separately.
- **The privacy escalation in the matchmaking step** — the reason it is gated behind a further proposal and an explicit, per-item consent step.
- **Critical mass** — if the memory launches empty or is never demonstrated, early value is too low to catch; hence seeding.
- **Evaluation at n≈14** — small, self-selected, short; the triangulated signals are the honest response, and none is reported as statistically significant.
- **The cost is new, recurring, and not small against the existing line.** About **£69 a month expected** — £19 for the server and £50 for the coop's compute — with a ceiling of about £94, against an infrastructure line that is currently £11 and total recurring costs of £344. That is a **20% increase in the Laboratory's recurring spending**, and it is the honest headline figure rather than the server cost alone. The sterling figures are indicative: the provider bills in dollars, and the conversion here follows the Record's own precedent, where `rule-cloud-server-hosting.md` prices the existing server at $14.40 a month and the Financial Policy books it as £11 — about $1.31 to the pound. No instrument fixes a rate, which is why the ceiling in clause 11.2 is set in dollars. The existing server cannot hold Emys — it has 2 GB with roughly 1 GB free — so this is a second server, not a resize. The ceiling is set at the 8 GB tier deliberately: the 4 GB figure is an estimate built from component sizes rather than a measurement of the assembled stack, and a ceiling means a resize does not cost the Laboratory a second deliberation. The Financial Policy's condition on additions to infrastructure is left intact and incorporated rather than amended, so adoption authorises the spending without bringing it forward.
- **A third party processes members' content** — inference happens on the East London GPU Coop's hardware (clause 8.1). Charter §8 reserves data protection to the College, and an arrangement where another organisation handles members' messages, however briefly and however little it logs, is the kind of thing the College's obligations reach. **The Record cannot settle this**: whether the College needs to be told, or to agree, is a question for the College, and this proposal does not decide it.
- **Dependence on software the Laboratory cannot fork** — the Laboratory is granted the use of a service and no copy of the engine (clause 4.2), so if the author stops maintaining Emys or the policy lapses, the Laboratory has its memory and no engine. The monthly export to a store on the Laboratory's own account, readable without the engine (clause 10.6), is what limits the damage; the engine is not something a successor cohort can pick up.
