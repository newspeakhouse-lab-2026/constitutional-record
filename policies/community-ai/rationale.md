# Rationale — Community AI (Emys)

*This document is not operative. It explains the reasoning behind the Emys policy and creates no duty of its own. Where it and the policy differ, the policy governs.*

## In the proposer's words

I have been working on CommClaw, a community memory and coordination bot for communities. CommClaw was already successfully deployed at TREEWEEK, a yearly community gathering I co-host.

I propose we name our instance **"Emys"**, a symbolic reversal of "Syme", the politically orthodox linguist and colleague of Winston from Orwel's 1984. Syme's job was to edit the Newspeak dictionary. Emys' job is to serve us in our governance journey by creating a living memory of our community, fostering the communal evolution of language and nuance, and encouraging flourishing neological deliberation, liberating thought and incubating new ideas.

You may have encountered [Emys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emys) in the wild, as it is also a species of [European turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_pond_turtle) :)

The engine is my project and will remain closed source for now, while I'm pursuing commercial deployments. What shapes how Emys behaves in our community — its prompts and configuration — is open and ours to change.

## The problem

Members report, in their own words, that it is hard to find things across the Discord: people do not know which channel to post in, information is easy to miss ("I put this in the Discord and you never saw it"), and nobody is sure who in the cohort could help with a given project. The Laboratory's own Collaboration brief names the same cluster — information arriving from many sources, "more possible choices, tasks, and decisions than any member can attend to", and offers and needs that members "often… not know what others have or want". Emys is one answer to that brief: make what is already in the channels findable, and make who-knows-what legible.

## Why this design

The design follows the research on shared group memory and on why such systems succeed or fail.

- **Lead with who-knows-what, not the fact store.** A [transactive memory system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactive_memory) is knowledge of *who knows what*, and that — not a central store of facts — is where the value lies (Wegner 1987, *Transactive Memory*; Ren & Argote 2011). Emys's highest-leverage function is helping members find the right *person*; the searchable fact-memory is the supporting, weaker half.
- **The bot does the work, so the people who benefit are not the people who curate.** [Groupware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaborative_software) most often dies from Grudin's disparity — those who must maintain the content are not those who gain from it (Grudin 1994, *Groupware and Social Dynamics*, Communications of the ACM). Emys ingests automatically; no member is asked to curate, which removes the commonest failure.
- **Success is measured in queries, not posts.** Most members consume and few contribute — the [90–9–1 rule of participation inequality](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/participation-inequality/) (Nielsen 2006; Leonardi, Huysman & Steinfield 2013). Because Emys generates the content itself, consumption with near-zero contribution is the intended mode, not a failure.
- **Always attribute the source.** An external store can make a group remember content less well — the [Google effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_effect) (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, *Science*, 2011). Attributing every fact to its source turns that from "the group forgets" into "the group learns who to ask" — the adaptive side of the same effect.
- **Be the lowest-effort path, with strong scent.** People use the cheapest route to an answer even at some cost to quality ([principle of least effort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_effort), Zipf 1949; [information foraging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_foraging), Pirolli & Card 1999). Emys must be cheaper than scrolling or asking a person, and must answer with a sourced snippet, not a bare link.
- **Seed it; value is interdependent.** The worth of a shared memory depends on others using it, so early value is low and can collapse below a threshold (Markus 1987, critical mass). The experiment begins with the memory pre-loaded and queries demonstrated, not empty.

## The honest limit

Lowering the cost of *finding* things changes findability; it does not, by itself, make people *participate*. Participation is a problem of stakes and incentives — a public-good information commons is under-provided absent selective incentives ([Olson 1965, *The Logic of Collective Action*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Logic_of_Collective_Action)). Emys can honestly promise *"easier to find what is already here, and who can help"*; it should not promise *"more engagement"*. If engagement is the goal, that is a separate lever — the member-contribution proposal, not this one.

## How we will judge it

Evaluation at this scale is genuinely hard (Grudin 1994, challenge 6), which is why clause 9.2 leans on three signals rather than one. Three things about reading them. In the usage record, watch *retrieval* rather than corpus size — an extraction loop can look healthy from the writing side while nobody ever reads it. Use in real work is the hardest signal to fake: a tool people merely like gets praised, while a tool people rely on leaves traces in decisions, posts and bookings. And the questionnaire is directional, not a significance test — at n≈14, report individual change, not p-values.

## The web workspace, and the matchmaking step that follows it

This experiment includes a **web workspace** because members will not do real work with an agent inside Discord — the workspace is where Emys earns its daily use, and daily use is what a shared memory needs to survive. The workspace also gives Emys its individual pay-off: a member comes to get their own work done, and the community value accrues as a by-product. This is the deliberate answer to Grudin's disparity.

The intended **matchmaking step** builds on that. As members work with Emys, their private workspace accumulates a picture of what each person knows and is working on. From that, Emys could proactively match people — *"someone else is working on something very close to this; you should talk"* — a far richer directory than any self-reported profile, and the strongest form of the who-knows-what value above. It is deliberately **not** authorised by this policy, for one reason: it would move private, work-derived information into shared memory, which inverts this experiment's central privacy guarantee. The policy forecloses it (clause 5.2) and names the guardrail it must meet — each contribution shown to and approved by the member before it is shared, with the AI suggesting and the member deciding. The data stays private until a later proposal, with that consent mechanism built, turns the feature on.

So matchmaking is split, deliberately. What a member *publishes* about themselves — their topic.forum profile — may seed the shared memory (clause 6.7), because they published it, it names its source, and they can have it removed. What they give Emys *in private* stays private. **The line is what the member has already chosen to make public, not what Emys could infer about them.**

## What we chose not to do

- **No member curation.** Requiring members to file knowledge would re-create Grudin's disparity; ingestion is automatic.
- **Facts, not opinions.** Opinions, complaints about people, and sensitive context are exactly the messy exceptions groupware handles badly (Grudin 1994), and the material most likely to chill participation if captured. Leaving them out is the privacy mitigation, aligns with the Record's "no new surveillance" constraint, and costs the tool little — factual community knowledge is what people actually search for.
- **Why one administrator, when §1 says no individual holds admin access alone.** Constitution §1 has two such sentences, and neither reaches Emys. The first reads: "The College holds administrative access to shared platforms until the Laboratory assumes it by Policy resolution (Layer 3). When the Laboratory holds access, no individual member holds it alone." The rule attaches to **access the Laboratory holds** — and the default it describes, the College holding admin until the Laboratory assumes it, only makes sense for platforms that predate the cohort, not for software a member wrote. The Laboratory assumes no administrative access to Emys (clause 2.3), so the sentence is not engaged. The second, in §1's *Computational tools* paragraph, binds a system that augments the **Agent role** — a clerical office for records and communications — which Emys is not. **The reading applied here is that §1's two-admin rule governs platforms the Laboratory administers, not a member's own tool that it uses.** The Record has not settled that. A member who reads §1 more broadly has a real reason to object; it is the strongest objection available to this proposal, and clause 2.3 is where to aim it.
- **Upgrades are the operator's to judge.** An earlier draft gated every new capability behind an Ordinary resolution and kept a register of live capabilities so the gate could be checked from outside. Both were dropped: a two-month experiment in which each feature waits on a deliberation period ships nothing, and the person building it is the person who can tell a bug fix from a new capability. The Laboratory gives up advance sight of changes nobody told it about, because whether something counts as substantial under clause 9.5 is one person's judgement. What remains closed is the pair that would actually matter: write access to the house instruments, and private material entering the shared memory.
- **Excluding a channel is a collective decision, not a member's right.** The alternative was letting any member pull a shared channel out of Emys unilaterally. Rejected because one person would be degrading a shared tool for thirteen others, and because a veto exercised after seeding leaves the memory in a state nobody agreed to. The cost lands on the member who wants a channel excluded: they must say so publicly and win a resolution. What makes it tolerable is that nobody has to argue for their *own* privacy — clause 10.4 takes a member's own material out, in whole or in part, at any time, without a vote or a reason.
- **The engine stays closed, and the Laboratory loses nothing it had.** Constitution §1 requires a member's computational tool to be documented, communicated and identified as AI — not open-sourced. (The open-weights requirement binds only a system augmenting the *Agent role*, which Emys is not; clause 8.1 commits to open weights anyway, by choice.) An earlier draft put Emys on Laboratory infrastructure and kept one administrator out of the source — protection that cost the Laboratory a hole in its own governance and gave the proposer only a promise. This design is cleaner: Emys is the proposer's service on infrastructure he rents (clauses 8.1, 11.1), and the Laboratory is granted its use rather than a copy (clause 4.2). Nothing is withheld from anyone because nothing is handed to anyone. The cost: one member holds the machine on which every member's workspace and messages sit, and clause 10.1(b) is observed by him rather than checked by anyone.
- **Whether Charter §3 reaches the engine.** Charter §3 devolves to the Laboratory "the channels, systems, and tools of its own creation". Under this design that argument is weak: the Laboratory does not authorise Emys into existence — §1 already permits a member to run an agent, and clause 1.1 says expressly that this policy is not such an authorisation — and it neither administers nor hosts it. It grants access to its own channels and URL (clause 2.2) and receives the use of a service (clause 4.2). The question is the College's to settle, not the Record's; clause 4.1 states the parties' intent, not a determination of the Charter.

## Anticipated objections, answered

- **"Our messages go to someone else's GPUs."** They do, for inference only; clause 8.1 has the arrangement. It is a deliberate trade. The Laboratory owns no GPUs capable of running a useful open-weights model, so the alternatives were a commercial AI provider — sending members' content to a company that logs and trains on it — or no Emys at all. A co-op that holds no logs and sells no data is the closest thing to house compute actually available. **What makes it acceptable is the no-logging undertaking, and the Laboratory cannot verify that from outside** — a reason to object, if a member thinks a third party's word is not enough.
- **"Why does one member get to keep the code, and what if he stops maintaining it?"** He wrote it before the Laboratory authorised anything and intends to reuse or sell it elsewhere. The Laboratory gets what it needs — a working tool, public prompts and configuration, the use of it for the experiment — without acquiring an asset it did not build. The exposure is real: granted a service and no copy of the engine (clause 4.2), if the author stops maintaining Emys or the policy lapses the Laboratory has its memory and no engine. The monthly export (clause 10.6) limits the damage; the engine is not something a successor cohort can pick up. **Not wanting to depend on software you cannot fork is the legitimate reason to object** — not the privacy clauses, which are unaffected.
- **"I don't want an AI reading our channels."** The limits in section 6 are the mitigation, and they are chosen rather than incidental — see *Facts, not opinions* above. The exits in the privacy declaration stay open to anyone unconvinced.
- **"I'd have to trust one person not to snoop."** Yes. Private messages and workspaces can never *enter* the shared memory, which is enforced by container isolation rather than by a promise. But the operator holds the machine, could technically read what is stored on it, and undertakes not to except to investigate a reported bug or incident (clause 10.1(b)). No other member can check that. One qualification runs the other way: because the web interface is served through the Laboratory's own server (clause 3.1), requests to it pass through infrastructure the operator does not solely control, and those who administer that server could see them. What stands behind the undertaking is a remedy rather than a check: section 10 is enforceable by the Laboratory, and the exits are listed in the privacy declaration. A member who would rather have two pairs of eyes than one person's word has a real reason to object.
- **"The real problem is our Discord is disorganised."** Partly true, and a separate job — better channel names — that this proposal neither does nor replaces. There is a risk in the other direction too: if finding things becomes easy, members may stop caring where they post, and the channels fragment further. Search treats the symptom; channel discipline is a norms problem, handled separately. But findability should not wait on a reorganisation nobody currently owns, and Emys covers far more: who-knows-what, the house systems, and a private workspace.

## Risks we are watching

- **The cost is new, recurring, and not small against the existing line.** Section 11 carries the figures. What matters for a decision is the comparison: the Laboratory's recurring costs are £344 a month and its entire infrastructure line is £11, so this is a material addition rather than a rounding error, and the honest headline is the total rather than the server alone. The existing house server cannot hold Emys — it has 2 GB with roughly 1 GB free — so this is a second server, not a resize. The ceiling sits a tier above the expected size on purpose, because the estimate is built from component sizes rather than measured on the assembled stack, and a ceiling means a resize does not cost the Laboratory a second deliberation. Against that, the compute is not bought for Emys alone: clause 8.5 opens the coop's capacity to members for their own work, so the £50 buys a cohort doing technical projects access to open-weights inference none of them has individually.
- **A third party processes members' content** — inference happens on the East London GPU Coop's hardware (clause 8.1). Charter §8 reserves data protection to the College, and an arrangement where another organisation handles members' messages, however briefly and however little it logs, is the kind of thing the College's obligations reach. **The Record cannot settle this**: whether the College needs to be told, or to agree, is a question for the College, and this proposal does not decide it.
- **The rate is flat but not fixed forever.** The coop agreed £50 a month without knowing the usage, and heavy load is a renegotiation rather than a cut-off: their load balancer slows requests rather than generating a bill, so the failure mode is Emys being slow, not a surprise cost. Recorded as an assumption on the proposal.
